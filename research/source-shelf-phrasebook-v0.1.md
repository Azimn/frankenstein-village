| Dorian Gray | 8.3 | Society snap |
| Invisible Man | 7.0 | Shortest. Inn-yard terseness |

**Village rule of thumb:** working folk and servants speak 7–9 words a sentence; gentry and professionals 10–13; nobody talks in letters (15+) except in letters. Sentence openers: Varney opens with *Oh,* and *Well,* (penny-dreadful); Dickens opens with *And* and *But* (working speech runs on); gentlemen open with *I* and *You*. A villager who opens every line with "Well," is a penny dreadful in disguise.

## 1. Per-novel speech profiles

### Frankenstein (Shelley, 1818) — the formal letter

Dialogue is scarce (333 segments — the novel is letters). What it gives is **formal written courtesy**, the register of requests, thanks, and refusals between gentry. Nobody in the village *talks* like this; everybody *writes* like this.

- Forms of address: *dear sir, my dear Victor, my dear father, good people*
- Oaths: *Good God, by heaven, Heaven forbid, God knows*
- Rhythm: longest in the corpus (15.0). Semicolons. "I have, doubtless, excited your curiosity…"
- Class note: no servant speech at all — the household is invisible. Use for letters, notices, the council's proclamations.

Quotable texture (ADAPT-ONLY — paraphrase, never paste):
1. "Will you have the kindness to inform me whither you are bound?" — *the formal inquiry; village use: gatekeeper, clerk, anyone asking business*
2. "I have, doubtless, excited your curiosity, as well as that of these good people; but you are too considerate to make inquiries." — *the polite non-answer; PERFECT deflection DNA — flatter the asker into silence*
3. "Certainly; it would indeed be very impertinent and inhuman in me to trouble you with any inquisitiveness of mine." — *apology after probing too deep*
4. "It gives me the greatest delight to see you; but tell me how you left my father, brothers, and Elizabeth." — *greeting pattern: delight, then the family inquiry*
5. "I did not before remark how very ill you appear; so thin and pale; you look as if you had been watching for several nights." — *noticing someone's state, gently*
6. "I will write instantly and relieve them from the anxiety they must feel." — *duty to write; the letter as care*
7. "Poor, poor girl, is she the accused? But it is wrongfully; every one knows that; no one believes it, surely?" — *defending the accused in company*
8. "My dear father, you are mistaken; she is innocent." — *plain protestation, no ornament*
9. "Dear sir, you are very kind to visit me; you, I hope, do not believe that I am guilty?" — *the accused's plea to a visitor*
10. "Heaven forbid! Even if you were really criminal, for that can only drive you to desperation, and not instigate you to virtue." — *moral reasoning aloud*
11. "By the by, I mean to lecture you a little upon their account myself." — *familiar teasing between friends*
12. "To go instantly to Geneva: come with me, Henry, to order the horses." — *decision spoken as itinerary*

### Dracula (Stoker, 1897) — the foreigner's English and the host's welcome

- Forms of address: *sir, madam, my friend, my good young friend, my dear, young Herr, Herr, Professor, Doctor, Mr. Harker*
- Oaths: *my God, I swear, good God, by God, God knows, bless me*
- Rhythm: 10.8. Van Helsing's signature: article-dropping Germanic syntax ("I have studied… I know…"), "my friend John / my friend Arthur" as punctuation.
- Class note: the Count's hospitality is the politest menace in the corpus. The tombstone-keeper ("Well, what else be they tombstones for? Answer me that, miss!") is working-man's retort at its best.

Quotable texture:
1. "Nay, sir, you are my guest. It is late, and my people are not available. Let me see to your comfort myself." — *the host's welcome; village use: any innkeeper, any invitation with teeth*
2. "Must you go? Oh! young Herr, must you go?" — *the foreign farewell; use for outsiders*
3. "Go on! You are first, and we shall follow; yours is the right to begin." — *deference to whoever leads*
4. "Because your peasant is at heart a coward and a fool!" — *Van Helsing's class bluntness; use for the educated dismissing the fearful*
5. "Because, dear sir, my coachman and horses are away on a mission." — *the polite excuse that explains nothing*
6. "I pray you, my good young friend, that you will not discourse of things other than business in your letters." — *letter etiquette as control*
7. "Come now and be silent. You shall kiss her once before it is done." — *tenderness inside a command; the hard kindness*
8. "We must trace each of these boxes; and when we are ready, we must either capture or kill this monster in his lair." — *planning cadence: inventory, then resolve*
9. "My life is hers, and I would give the last drop of blood in my body for her." — *the devotion vow (ADAPT-ONLY — famous-adjacent)*
10. "Well, what else be they tombstones for? Answer me that, miss!" — *working man's retort; the churchyard keeper's whole character in one line*
11. "I am younger and stronger, Professor. It must be me." — *volunteering, no flourish*
12. "There, I knew the poor old chap would get into some kind of trouble; didn't I say it all along?" — *gossip's vindication*
13. "Bother them all! I don't care a pin about them." — *bluster; "I don't care a pin" is pure period*
14. "Shut the door; I shall wait till morning!" — *resolve, four words*

### Carmilla (Le Fanu, 1872) — the intimate dread

- Forms of address: *dear, darling, papa, sir, my lady, Madame, Baron*
- Oaths: rare — *by God, bless me, "Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake"*
- Rhythm: 13.2, languorous. Fear expressed as manners: "Tell me nothing about ghosts. I shan't sleep tonight if you do."
- Class note: aristocratic dismissal of peasants ("She? I don't trouble my head about peasants") — the manor voice.

Quotable texture:
1. "Lay your hand along that hollow in the bed; someone did lie there, so sure as you did not; the place is still warm." — *dreadful noticing (ADAPT-ONLY — famous scene; take the shape: the physical detail that proves the impossible)*
2. "I am afraid he is in great affliction; the letter appears to me to have been written very nearly in distraction." — *reading emotion out of a letter*
3. "I cannot do that, sir, it would be to task your kindness and chivalry too cruelly." — *refusal by flattery — deflection DNA*
4. "Don't approach, one at a time is as much as she can at present converse with." — *the sickroom rule*
5. "Did you remark what an ill-looking pack of men the servants were?" — *noticing the staff; suspicion sideways*
6. "Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since." — *dream-memory as fact*
7. "Good night, darling, it is very hard to part with you, but good night; tomorrow, but not early, I shall see you again." — *the intimate farewell*
8. "I hope there is no plague or fever coming; all this looks very like it." — *foreboding as small talk*
9. "She? I don't trouble my head about peasants. I don't know who she is." — *aristocratic dismissal; the manor's whole attitude*
10. "My father has gone on with the clergyman to the churchyard. I thought you knew she was to be buried today." — *funeral news delivered sideways*
11. "I have got into one of my moping moods tonight." — *"moping moods" — period melancholy, no psychology-talk*
12. "Who was ever being so born to calamity?" — *self-pity with grammar*

### Jekyll & Hyde (Stevenson, 1886) — the lawyer and the servant

- Forms of address: *sir, Mr. Utterson, Dr. Lanyon, Poole, my dear*
- Oaths: sparse — *by the look of him, Hold your tongue!*
- Rhythm: 9.2, legal precision. Utterson never wastes a word; Poole the butler is the corpus's best servant (deferential, exact, quietly terrified).
- Class note: master–servant probing — "Your master seems to repose a great deal of trust in that young man, Poole" answered by "O, dear no, sir. He never dines here." The dodge is the whole relationship.

Quotable texture:
1. "I am ashamed of my long tongue. Let us make a bargain never to refer to this again." — *sealing a secret between gentlemen*
2. "You know me: I am a man to be trusted. Make a clean breast of this in confidence." — *"make a clean breast" — the invitation to confess*
3. "I want you to hear, and I don't want you to be heard. And see here, sir, if by any chance he was to ask you in, don't go." — *careful instructions; the conditional warning*
4. "Will you wait here by the fire, sir? or shall I give you a light in the dining room?" — *the servant's offer; hospitality as procedure*
5. "O, dear no, sir. He never dines here." — *the servant's deflection; answers the question beside the question*
6. "I did not think you would have lied." — *the quiet reproach; worse than shouting*
7. "And you never asked about the—place with the door?" — *drawing someone out*
8. "What sort of a man is he to see?" — *asking for a description, plainly*
9. "With all my heart." — *assent, three words*
10. "My mind misgives me he is in deep waters!" — *"my mind misgives me" — period foreboding, no "I feel like"*
11. "If any one knows, it will be Lanyon." — *deferring to the expert*
12. "I let my brother go to the devil in his own way." — *resignation as policy*

### The Picture of Dorian Gray (Wilde, 1890) — society snap

- Forms of address: *my dear fellow, my dear boy, Harry, Basil, sir, Lord Henry, Lady Agatha, Mr. Gray*
- Oaths: *my God, good heavens, upon my word, good God*
- Rhythm: 8.3 — the snappiest gentry talk in the corpus. Contradiction as conversation.
- Class note: one servant line does all the work — "Mr. Dorian Gray is in the studio, sir." The lower-class oath ("as sure as there is a God in heaven, if he ever does you any wrong, I shall kill him") is James Vane — menace without manners.
- **Tic warning:** Wilde's epigrams ("Nothing can cure the soul but the senses…"). Villagers must never aphorize. The epigram is Lord Henry's alone, and he isn't in the village.

Quotable texture:
1. "My dear fellow, I congratulate you most warmly." — *society congratulation; the warmth is the form*
2. "Upon my word, Dorian, Miss Sibyl knows how to pay compliments." — *society talk; "upon my word" does the lifting*
3. "I am in Lady Agatha's black books at present." — *"in someone's black books" — social disfavor, pure period*
4. "Dorian's whims are laws to everybody, except himself." — *remarking on a patron (ADAPT the shape, never the epigram)*
5. "It would be rather hard lines on your work." — *"hard lines" — sympathy idiom*
6. "I don't agree with a single word that you have said, and, what is more, Harry, I feel sure you don't either." — *drawing-room contradiction*
7. "If you let any one have it but me, Basil, I shall never forgive you!" — *possessive demand, no politeness*
8. "Do come in. The light is quite perfect, and you can bring your drinks." — *hospitality; the detail sells it*
9. "I should have objected very strongly this morning, Lord Henry." — *formal objection, belated*
10. "Then you shall come; and you will come, too, Basil, won't you?" — *social pressure, lightly applied*
11. "Mr. Dorian Gray is in the studio, sir." — *the servant's announcement; name, place, "sir", done*

### The Time Machine (Wells, 1895) — DUD for speech, two keeps

Dialogue is 192 segments of dinner-party science debate. Almost nothing a villager would say — except:
1. "But wait until tomorrow. Wait for the common sense of the morning." — *KEEP. "The common sense of the morning" is village wisdom; the well-keeper could say this.*
2. "Are you perfectly serious? Or is this a trick — like that ghost you showed us last Christmas?" — *KEEP as skepticism template: "Is this a trick — like that [thing] you [did] last [time]?"*
3. "It would be remarkably convenient for the historian." — *dry understatement pattern.*

### The Invisible Man (Wells, 1897) — the inn-yard

The best lower-class speech in the corpus after Dickens. Mrs. Hall's inn, the constable, the villagers — suspicion made audible. Heavy apostrophe-dialect in print ('tas, 'en, 'e); **normalize to light flavor for game use** (drop half the apostrophes or it becomes unreadable).

- Forms of address: *sir, ma'am, my good woman*
- Oaths: *Bless my soul alive!*
- Rhythm: 7.0 — shortest in the corpus. Terseness is the class marker.
- Class note: the money talk is exact — "A shilling—put down a shilling. Surely a shilling's enough?", "You told me three days ago that you hadn't anything but a sovereign's worth of silver upon you.", "I told you three days ago I was awaiting a remittance—" (the debtor's stall).

Quotable texture (lightly normalized):
1. "In the name of human charity! A room and a fire!" — *the desperate plea; need as invocation*
2. "What a turn them bandages did give me, to be sure!" — *gossip's shock ("them bandages" — keep the grammar slip, it IS the voice)*
3. "Would you mind, sir, this man coming to look at the clock, sir?" — *the deferential request; "sir" twice*
4. "I wish you wouldn't come in without knocking." — *the boundary, plainly stated*
5. "Certainly, sir. You can turn the lock if you're like that, you know. Any time." — *the landlady's permission; "if you're like that" is perfect*
6. "Don't. If the straw makes trouble put it down in the bill." — *practical innkeeping*
7. "It's a rummy case altogether." — *"rummy" = odd; period slang, use sparingly*
8. "We'd best be taking his luggage in." — *work talk; the decision made aloud*
9. "I have to arrest him, and arrest him I will." — *the constable's determination (normalized from dialect)*
10. "He was afraid, one time, that he'd have to have an operation — he was that bad, sir." — *"he was that bad" — intensifier pattern*
11. "Looks a bit like a disguise, don't it?" — *suspicion as a question*
12. "I know it's spirits. I've read in papers of them. Tables and chairs leaping and dancing." — *the newspaper as authority*

### The Island of Dr. Moreau (Wells, 1896) — shipboard and the evasive definition

- Forms of address: *sir, Captain, Mr. Prendick, Montgomery*
- Oaths: *Damn that howling! Why the devil don't you get out of the way?*
- Rhythm: 7.0, blunt. Montgomery loosens only in starlight.
- Class note: the captain's authority ("Do what I like on my own ship") vs. Montgomery's drunk candor.

Quotable texture:
1. "I'd advise you to keep your hands off him." — *the warning; no reason given*
2. "There's something in this starlight that loosens one's tongue. I'm an ass, and yet somehow I would like to tell you." — *the confession opener; setting as excuse*
3. "Whatever you tell me, you may rely upon my keeping to myself — if that's it." — *the promise of secrecy*
4. "I must apologise for that. Now you are our guest, we must make you comfortable — though you are uninvited, you know." — *the host's apology with the barb left in*
5. "That alters the case a little, Mr. Prendick." — *reassessment, aloud*
6. "As it happens, we are biologists here. This is a biological station — of a sort." — *the evasive definition; "of a sort" undoes the sentence — THE village pattern for talking about the Mists, the well, the truce*
7. "You'll find this island an infernally rum place, I promise you. I'd watch my goings carefully, if I were you." — *the warning with the pronoun dropped*
8. "What an ass I was to mention it to you! Anyhow, it will give you an inkling of our — mysteries. Whiskey?" — *THE deflection: self-reproach, then a drink. Redirect with refreshment.*
9. "Your sailors began to haze the poor devil as soon as he came aboard." — *complaint on another's behalf*
10. "I'm itching to get to work again — with this new stuff." — *appetite, plainly stated*

### The Woman in White (Collins, 1859) — the deposition and the conspirator

- Forms of address: *sir, miss, Mr. Hartright, Miss Halcombe, Miss Fairlie, my love, Walter*
- Oaths: restrained — *Upon my word, good heavens*
- Rhythm: 9.8, legal-deposition clarity. Collins's people state things for the record even in speech.
- Class note: Fosco's Italian-English ("A thousand pardons, Mr. Hartright, I'm afraid I bore you") — the foreigner's elaborate courtesy. The servant's anxious confession ("I hope there was no harm in it — I hope I didn't do wrong") is the period servant in one line.
- **Tic warning:** Collins's legalism ("I proceed to…", "in the interests of truth"). Use for the clerk and the council; nowhere else.

Quotable texture:
1. "Mentioned my mother's name! You interest me indescribably. Pray go on." — *hooked attention; "Pray go on" is the period "tell me more"*
2. "A thousand pardons, Mr. Hartright, I'm afraid I bore you." — *the elaborate apology; foreign courtesy*
3. "I beg your pardon, Miss Halcombe. I feel the strongest possible concern and interest in anything that affects Miss Fairlie's happiness or yours." — *formal concern, fully dressed*
4. "Hush, hush! She is coming of her own accord. Say nothing in her presence. Let this discovery be kept a secret between you and me." — *the conspiracy instruction*
5. "Not to be thought of for a moment." — *refusal, absolute, six words*
6. "May I venture to inquire why you express that hope?" — *the careful question*
7. "The winter is coming — ah, my love, the winter is coming soon!" — *foreboding as tenderness*
8. "Once again, I am glad you escaped — I am glad you prospered well after you left me." — *the reunion; repetition is the feeling*
9. "Come, come! This resolves itself into a matter of bargaining after all. What is the least you will take?" — *the negotiation; "after all" concedes the game*
10. "Yes, miss — I told them Sir Percival was coming. I hope there was no harm in it — I hope I didn't do wrong." — *the servant's anxious confession*
11. "Don't look at me like that. Speak to me — tell me what you want." — *demand for plainness*
12. "My departure must be a very early one." — *the formal farewell notice*
13. "Upon my word, Mr. Dempster, you pay my feelings a great compliment in thinking them weak enough to be shocked by such an urchin as that!" — *the ironic compliment; use once a year*

### Bleak House & Great Expectations (Dickens, 1852/1860) — the crowd

The deepest mine: 10,700 dialogue segments between them. Dickens gives every class a distinct mouth.

- Forms of address: *sir, ma'am, miss, my dear, my man, young master, governor, your honour (rare)*
- Oaths: *dear me, good gracious, bless my soul, by heaven, upon my word, upon my soul, good Lord, God knows*
- Rhythm: 9.0 / 8.4. Working speech runs on *And* and *But* openers; agreement is "To be sure, to be sure!", "No doubt, no doubt."
- Class note: Joe's "I say, you know!" and "what larks!" (famous — ADAPT-ONLY); Magwitch's "you know what wittles is?"; Mrs. Joe's "fret and fright"; Jaggers's cross-examination; the gatekeeper's "It's opposed to my orders to hold the gate open."
- **Tic warning:** Dickensian triple-repetition ("Fog everywhere…"). One triplet per novel is a homage; two is a tic.

Great Expectations texture:
1. "If you can cough any trifle on it up, Pip, I'd recommend you to do it." — *hinting for money without asking*
2. "Don't straggle, my man. Close up here." — *"my man" — authority to a subordinate, not unkind*
3. "Tell me directly what you've been doing to wear me away with fret and fright." — *the scolding; "fret and fright" is the music*
4. "There are five-and-twenty guineas in this bag. Give it to your master, Pip." — *the money handoff; exact sum, exact instruction*
5. "Ah, young master, there's more changes than yours. But come in, come in. It's opposed to my orders to hold the gate open." — *the gatekeeper; kindness inside the rule*
6. "What'll you drink? You didn't mention your name, by the bye." — *the tavern ask*
7. "If a man is not his own neighbour, who is?" — *Joe-shaped proverb; the village needs a dozen of these*
8. "Hear me out — but if I were to remove Joe into a higher sphere…" — *the awkward proposal; good intentions, bad grammar of feeling*
9. "Don't lose your temper. Leave this lad to me, ma'am; leave this lad to me." — *the intervention; repetition is the grip*
10. "Why don't you cry again, you little wretch?" — *the taunt*
11. "I say, you know!" — *Joe's discourse marker; contentless and perfect*
12. "You know what wittles is?" — *(normalize spelling in game use) the test question*

Bleak House texture:
1. "To be sure, to be sure!" — *agreement doubled*
2. "Bless my soul!" — *the oath; surprise, dismay, delight — does everything*
3. "Mind you do it thoroughly, and don't loiter. Shirking won't do for me. Make haste! Go along!" — *the foreman's orders; "Shirking won't do for me"*
4. "Come, come, put a brighter face upon it!" — *cheering up; "Come, come" + name is the period arm round the shoulder*
5. "You'll excuse my asking again if it has anything to do with a picture, ma'am." — *the careful question*
6. "But would you allow me the favour of a minute's private conversation?" — *requesting privacy, formally*
7. "You will find that the place is rough, sir." — *warning a gentleman downward*
8. "An extremely dangerous person in any community. A man of a very low character of mind." — *the denunciation; "low character of mind"*
9. "Oh, go on, do!" — *encouragement*
10. "What a strange house!" — *the arrival remark; use at every threshold*
11. "If it wasn't that we had been intimate when you stopped at our house—" — *the claim of acquaintance, trailing off*
12. "I was a ward myself. I was not mad at that time." — *the sly aside (ADAPT-ONLY)*

### The Sherlock Holmes stories (Doyle) — the professional interview

Mined across three texts (Adventures, Hound, Study in Scarlet). The register is **the consulting professional**: interviews, deductions, appointments.

- Forms of address: *sir, Doctor, Watson, Holmes, Mr. Holmes, my dear fellow, your Majesty, Sir Henry, Dr. Mortimer, madam*
- Oaths: *good heavens, my God, by Jove, great heavens, good God, dear me*
- Rhythm: ~10. Brisk, interrogative. "Pray" as softener (41 hits in Adventures: "Pray take a seat", "Pray go on").
- Class note: Barrymore the butler ("Very good, sir. If I must I must."), the cabman ("I dare say my fare knew all about it"), Hugo Baskerville's sons told to "say nothing".

Quotable texture:
1. "Pray take a seat, Sir Henry. Do I understand you to say that you have yourself had some remarkable experience since you arrived in London?" — *the interview opening; "Do I understand you to say" is the professional's grip*
2. "I am much obliged to you for bringing us together." — *thanks, formal*
3. "Which would suit us down to the ground." — *"down to the ground" — agreement idiom*
4. "You don't mind the smell of strong tobacco, I hope?" — *the host's check*
5. "I shall be at Baker Street at ten." — *the appointment; place + hour, no verb wasted*
6. "It is a little off the beaten track, isn't it?" — *"off the beaten track" — the remark*
7. "This is very piquant." — *reaction, one word doing the work*
8. "Look here, Barrymore—" — *direct address + name; the summons*
9. "Hush — my brother is coming." — *(normalized) the warning; "Not a word" variant: "Can you not tell when a warning is for your own good?"*
10. "What a pity! He might throw some light on that which is so dark to us." — *"throw some light on" — the regret*
11. "There are certainly one or two indications upon the stick. It gives us the basis for several deductions." — *the deduction cadence; "indications", "basis"*
12. "You remind me of Edgar Allan Poe's Dupin." — *the literary comparison (village version: "You remind me of [person]")*
13. "Do you include violin-playing in your category of rows?" — *dry humor as a question*
14. "A proposition which I took the liberty of doubting." — *polite dissent (ADAPT-ONLY — famous-adjacent)*

### The Great God Pan (Machen, 1894) — thin mine, five keeps

Dialogue is sparse (245 segments) and mostly antiquarian murmur. Five usable:
1. "Consider the matter well. It's a great responsibility. Something might go wrong; you would be a miserable man for the rest of your days." — *warning a friend off*
2. "Are you perfectly sure that your theory is not a phantasmagoria — a splendid vision, certainly, but a mere vision after all?" — *the skeptical challenge*
3. "It's a long story, and a strange one too, but you can hear it if you like." — *the story offer*
4. "I have lost sight of him for many years." — *losing touch, plainly*
5. "I sold it all; the fields and woods, the dear old house — everything." — *ruin, stated without self-pity*

### Varney the Vampire (Prest/Rymer, 1845–47) — the penny dreadful

The largest dialogue mine (17,447 segments) and the most melodramatic. Rich in oaths, deference, and committee-speak; use at quarter strength or it becomes pastiche.

- Forms of address: *sir, ma'am, Mr. Marchdale, brother, your honour, Flora, Jack*
- Oaths: *Heaven knows, by heaven, I swear, for the love of truth, On your honour*
- Rhythm: 12.6 — swell and repetition. Opens lines with *Oh,* and *Well,* more than any other novel.
- Class note: "Yes, sir, it was a vampyre." — the servant's plain testimony is the best line in the book. "As you please, brother, and as you please, Mr. Marchdale." — deference doubled.
- **Tic warning:** melodramatic repetition ("horror! horror!", "Do not, oh, do not"). One per scene maximum.

Quotable texture:
1. "Give me leave to think of it until to-morrow." — *asking for time, formally*
2. "Heaven knows we have mysteries enough in our family." — *family resignation; "Heaven knows" + "enough"*
3. "It would take much more to convince me." — *the skeptic's wall*
4. "Well, Marchdale, what think you of this new and extraordinary episode in our affairs?" — *consulting a friend; "what think you"*
5. "Then I say we believe, as far as human judgment has a right to go, that a vampyre has been here." — *the committee conclusion (ADAPT — "as far as human judgment has a right to go")*
6. "Heaven knows that this place is becoming daily more hateful to us all." — *the house oppressing; "daily more hateful"*
7. "Do not make his lonely thoughts terrible with more than that. Tell him I am mad." — *the protective lie*
8. "For the love of truth, tell me!" — *"for the love of truth" — the demand*
9. "On your honour." — *the binding oath; two words*
10. "Yes, sir, it was a vampyre." — *the servant's plain testimony; no ornament, therefore believed*
11. "I will think of it. About such a proposition I would not decide hastily." — *deliberation aloud*

## 2. Cross-novel phrasebook by USE

*Each entry: the phrase/pattern → novels → who says it in the village. Patterns in SMALL CAPS are templates, not quotes.*

### Greetings

- "Good day to you." / "Good morning / afternoon / evening." → all — *the default; "How do you do?" follows, never "How are you?"*
- "How do you do?" → Holmes, Dickens — *the period handshake in words; said on meeting AND on being introduced*
- "Come in, come in." → Dickens, Invisible Man, Dorian Gray — *the warm welcome; doubled verb*
- "I am glad you came — very glad indeed." → Great God Pan — *the sincere welcome; "indeed" seals it*
- "Do come in. The light is quite perfect." → Dorian Gray — *hospitality with a detail*
- "Hullo." → Wells — *informal, working-class, male; ADOPT as the period "hey" — never "hello" (0 hits in the corpus), never "hi"*
- "May I come in?" → Bleak House — *asking entry*
- "Nay, sir, you are my guest." → Dracula — *the host asserting hospitality over the guest's protest*
- "Ah, young master, there's more changes than yours. But come in, come in." → Great Expectations — *the gatekeeper's greeting; recognition + rule + welcome*

### Farewells

- "Farewell." → all — *weighty; not casual*
- "Good night." / "Good-bye." → all — *ordinary*
- "Adieu." → rare, literary — *reserve for the dramatic*
- "Until we meet again." → pattern — *the promise inside the goodbye*
- "I trust that we shall soon have some good news for you." → Holmes — *the professional's farewell; forward-looking*
- "My departure must be a very early one." → Woman in White — *the formal notice*
- "Good night — it is very hard to part with you, but good night; tomorrow, but not early, I shall see you again." → Carmilla — *the intimate farewell*
- "Must you go? Oh! must you go?" → Dracula — *the reluctant farewell*

### Thanks

- "I thank you." / "I am much obliged to you." → Holmes, Dickens — *the standard; "much obliged" is warmer than "thank you"*
- "A thousand thanks." → Varney — *stronger; the thousand is the measure*
- "How can I thank you?" → Carmilla — *the overwhelmed thanks*
- "You are very kind." → Frankenstein — *thanks as compliment*
- "Say nothing, then — a thousand thanks." → Varney — *thanks inside secrecy*

### Refusals and deflections

- "I cannot." / "I will not." / "I dare not." → all — *the plain refusal; "dare not" admits fear, "will not" asserts will*
- "It is not to be thought of for a moment." → Woman in White — *absolute, six words*
- "Impossible." / "Out of the question." → all — *the wall*
- "It is no use." / "There is no use." → all — *resignation as refusal*
- "You are too considerate to make inquiries." → Frankenstein — *THE deflection: flatter the asker into silence*
- "It would be to task your kindness too cruelly." → Carmilla — *refusal by flattery*
- "I have no right to ask." → Woman in White — *withdrawing the question*
- "O, dear no, sir. He never dines here." → Jekyll & Hyde — *the servant's dodge: answer the question beside the question*
- "Some things you maintain without understanding." → *(village original, NPC banks)* — *listed here because the SHELF shape is "ask the priest — he reads them; I just keep them clean" (well-keeper pattern)*
- "What an ass I was to mention it! Anyhow — [DRINK]?" → Moreau — *self-reproach, then refreshment; redirect with hospitality*
- "He stands salt water well" → Varney — *praise that ends inquiry*

### Oaths and exclamations (class-graded)

Gentry: *by Jove, good heavens, great heavens, good God, upon my word, upon my soul, upon my honour, Heaven forbid, God knows*
Middle: *my God, good Lord, good gracious, bless me, dear me, I swear*
Working: *bless my soul (alive!), dear me, Lord love us, mercy on us*
Rough: *damn, devil, hell* — *note: print euphemized as "d—d"; spoken freely but rarely in the novels. A villager who swears like a sailor should be rare enough to notice.*
- "Bless my soul!" → Bleak House — *does everything: surprise, dismay, delight*
- "Heaven knows." → Varney — *resignation; "Heaven knows we have mysteries enough"*
- "For the love of truth!" → Varney — *the demand*
- "On your honour." → Varney — *the binding oath; two words*
- "My mind misgives me." → Jekyll & Hyde — *foreboding without "I feel like"*
- "As sure as there is a God in heaven." → Dorian Gray — *the lower-class vow*

### Weather talk

- "The night air is chill." → Dracula — *weather as warning*
- "The white mist gathering over the river was chilly." → Great God Pan — *weather as mood*
- "The winter is coming — ah, the winter is coming soon!" → Woman in White — *weather as foreboding*
- "Rain don't hurt the well." → *(village original)* — *the shelf shape: "What a turn them bandages did give me" is gossip; weather talk is work talk in disguise (voice guide rule 9)*
- Pattern: WEATHER + WORK-CONSEQUENCE — *"Frost on the rim. I break it at dawn." The novels moralize weather; the village should monetize it.*

### Money talk

- "Five-and-twenty guineas." → Great Expectations — *exact sums, spoken aloud; "five-and-twenty" not "twenty-five"*

- "If you please, sir, I should wish to speak to you." → Great Expectations — *money talk begins with deference*
- Coins: *guinea, sovereign, pound, shilling, pence, penny, farthing* — *never "bucks", never "cash" as a noun for money on hand ("ready money" is the period term)*

### Deference (the servant's grammar)

- "Yes, sir." / "No, sir." / "Yes, miss." / "Yes, ma'am." → all — *the whole relationship in two words*
- "If you please, sir." → Dickens, Varney — *prefaces any request upward*
- "Begging your pardon." / "I beg your pardon." → all — *the all-purpose softener: interruption, disagreement, apology*
- "As you please." → Varney, Moreau — *agreement that keeps its reservations*
- "Very good, sir." → Hound — *the order accepted*
- "Shall I…?" → all — *"Shall I open the coffin?" — the servant's offer is always a question*
- "Your honour." → Varney — *for magistrates and masters; not for the gentry*
- "Pray." → Holmes, Woman in White — *"Pray take a seat", "Pray go on" — the gentleman's softener*
- "With your permission." / "If I may." → all — *asking leave*
- "Will you wait here by the fire, sir? or shall I give you a light?" → Jekyll & Hyde — *the choice offered upward*

## 3. Anachronism flags

### True negatives — modern words with ZERO hits across all 16 texts

Never let these into village speech: **okay / O.K., hello, hi, hey, gonna, wanna, guys** (as address), **creepy, vibe, trauma** (psych sense), **weird** (as slang — the novels use it only as "eerie"), **stuff** (as vague noun — novels use "stuff and nonsense" or materials), **cool** (as approval — novels use it for temperature/composure only), **like** (as quotative/filler), **basically, whatever** (dismissive — novels use "whatever" only as "nothing whatever").

### False friends — verified period-correct, safe to use

- **hullo** — the informal greeting (Wells). ADOPT as the period "hey".
- **ain't** — lower-class period speech (Dickens, Wells). Keep to servants and roughs.
- **cool** — temperature and composure ("very cool" = unflappable). Safe.
- **whatever** — only as "nothing whatever", "whatever they may be". Never dismissive.
- **you know** — period uses are contentful ("You know what wittles is?", "I say, you know!"). Ban the pure-filler modern "you know".
- **I mean** — period form is "I mean to say". Ban the softener.
- **check** — chess, restraining, "check to the King". Never the bill (that's "the reckoning" or "the bill").
- **candy** — a surname in Dracula (Mitchell, Sons, & Candy). The sweet is "sweets".
- **kid** — leather (kid gloves). The child is "child", "lad", "lass", "boy", "girl".
- **guy** — a Guy Fawkes effigy. The fellow is "fellow", "chap", "man".
- **telephone** — existed (1 hit each in 3 novels); rare enough to feel wrong in a village. Avoid.
- **gotten** — 4 hits, all Varney (1840s). Archaic by the 1890s; avoid.
- **contact** — only "in contact with". Never "contact him" (verb). Use "get word to", "send to", "find".
- **mad** — means insane, never angry ("Are you mad?" = "Are you insane?"). For anger: "angry", "in a temper", "furious".
- **sick** — nauseated. For ill: "ill", "unwell", "poorly".
- **pants** — underwear. The garment is "trousers".
- **fall** — the season is "autumn".
- **sidewalk** — "pavement". **store** — "shop". **date** (romantic) — postdates; use "appointment", "engagement".
- **sort of / kind of** — "a sort of" is period (Moreau). Allow sparingly.

### Author tics — cap, don't ban

- **Wilde:** the epigram. No villager aphorizes. Ever.
- **Dickens:** the triple-repetition. One per novel is homage; two is a tic.
- **Stoker:** timestamping everything; Van Helsing's broken English — reserve Germanic syntax for actual foreigners.
- **Varney:** melodramatic repetition ("Do not, oh, do not"). Quarter strength or pastiche.
- **Collins:** legalism ("I proceed…", "in the interests of truth"). The clerk and the council only.
- **Wells:** apostrophe-dialect ('tas, 'en). Normalize to light flavor — half the apostrophes, maximum.
- **Shelley:** nobody talks in letters. Letters talk in Shelley.

### Print conventions that aren't speech

- "d—d" and "G—d" dashes are print euphemism, not pronunciation. A character saying "damn" says "damn".
- Italics for emphasis ("_he_ can tell") become spoken stress, not air quotes.
- Chapter-ending aphorisms are narration, not dialogue. Don't give them to NPCs.
