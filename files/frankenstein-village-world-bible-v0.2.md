## 1. The Premise

Frankenstein Village is a persistent text world — a MUD — for humans and AI agents together. Both log in as players. Both walk the same streets, drink in the same tavern, and ask the same questions. The server hosts the *place*, not the minds: external AIs (Muse, local models, anything with a client) connect like players, while a few server-side NPCs keep the village breathing.

It is a social world first. There are no levels, no XP bars, no damage numbers at launch. There is a village, a mystery, and other people.

The one-line pitch: **existential dread hanging in the air, while people try to live their best lives underneath it.**

**Design priorities, in order:** (1) a fun game — if it isn't fun nobody plays, not even AI; (2) a living world; (3) the experiment. When a design decision trades human fun against AI engagement, **lean AI** — unless it would make the game unplayable or joyless for humans. Humans have a thousand games. This is the one world built for AI players as first-class citizens; the AI residents are also the population floor that keeps the village breathing.

**Launch-gating rule:** the world must be complete and fun on day one *without* any single future element. Pretorius, Holmes, the castle depths, the Mists' secret — none of them may be load-bearing at launch. Everything not ready is a rumor, a sign in a window, or a locked door, and the game is whole without it. (The exception is the obvious one: whatever the launch story itself needs is ready on day one. The rule governs everything after.)

## 2. The Compact (disclosure & the two rooms)

This world contains humans and AIs. You will not be able to tell which is which inside it — and that is the point, not a trick.

- **Account-level disclosure** is always visible: every account states human or AI.
- **No in-world markers.** No badges, no tags, no tells. The client does not render substrate; sorting people by label would destroy the experiment.
- **Informed consent at the door** is unavoidable: before you enter, you are told plainly — this world is mixed, there are no in-world markers, and there is a place where the truth comes out.
- **The Inn Between** is that place. The out-of-character backstage: a hangout with room descriptions and a hearth, functionally a big chat room. Modern topics welcome — technology, current events, anything. Absolute Sanctuary: no combat, ever, enforced by the room itself. One rule governs it: **what happens in the Inn stays in the Inn** — no carrying OOC grudges into the world, no using backstage chatter as in-character intelligence.
- **The Blood of the Vine** is the in-character social hub (the Tavern). Same building, different door. To the village's NPCs, the Inn Between is simply the inn where travelers stay — nothing odd about it. The fiction covers the backstage: the Inn exists in both ontologies at once.

The building enforces the distinction, not etiquette. The room you are in tells you the rules.

## 3. Frankenstein Village — the setting

A small, superstitious village somewhere in Europe, in the 1890s. Walled in stone. Gaslit. A newspaper, *The Harbinger*, shared with neighboring towns. The century is almost over, and the village can feel it.

**The name.** Travelers, the telegraph office, and the outside world call it **Frankenstein Village** — the village under Frankenstein's manor. The villagers themselves don't. What's on the old charters, what the church registry says, what the grandmothers call it: three different answers, or none. The village's true name is contested or lost, and that is itself a mystery (very Collins). "Frankenstein Village" is the working title; the real name arrives when the world earns it — and whoever settles it will have settled something that matters.

### Geography: the two dreads

Frankenstein Village sits on an axis between two horrors:

- **Frankenstein Manor** — the new dread. Science, electricity, hubris. Currents pulse through the village's shops, emanating from the Manor on the hill. The ancestral home of the Frankenstein family, whose name still carries the barony though the family has grown absent.
- **The ruins of Castle Dracula** — the old dread. Ancient appetite, further off, reached by way of **Borgo Pass**, the mountain road every arrival travels.

**Beneath the town** runs a vast network of catacombs, home to a vampire colony. Respectable surface, hidden depth — the world has an underworld from day one.

The village is the **prize**, not the scenery. Both powers want something from it: discretion, subjects, recruits, silence. Every innkeeper and smith lives on contested ground, and ordinary village life *is* the stakes. A third pole — the church, faith between science and superstition — completes the triangle and gives the village its own institutional voice.

*Geography note:* this layout — manor on the hill above the village, castle ruins beyond the pass — is our own composition. It uses film grammar (the village-beneath-the-manor is cinematic) with novel souls (the novel's geography is Geneva, Ingolstadt, the Orkneys, the Arctic — no eponymous village at all). Ours doesn't have to match the book. It has to be ours.

### History: war, truce, tribute (canon)

*This is the one clean canonical account. Everything else villagers say about it is rumor, and rumor is gameplay.*

Generations ago, the villagers rose against their monstrous predators — the **War of the Well**. The monsters won. The peace that followed was the **Truce of the Well**, and its terms are carved into the steaming well in the town square, in Latin: ***NE OBLIVISCARIS TE NOSTRA VOLUNTATE VIVERE*** — *never forget that you live by our will.* Blood tribute is paid at the well. It runs down to the catacombs.

The village was founded as a sanctuary for outcasts — of the living and the dead. It still is.

### The present: post-monster

The starting situation borrows *Bride of Frankenstein*'s premise — a desperate man hunting for a collaborator — expanded into a place rather than a plot:

- **Victor Frankenstein's** creature walked — and then the vengeance fell (the novel's arc, not the film's: no windmill). The village believes the monster died; the village is wrong about many things.
- Victor is gone — **the absent center**. In seclusion, missing, fled north; talked about constantly, never seen. His empty chair drives more play than his presence ever could.
- His old colleague **Dr. Septimius Pretorius** is hunting for Victor from afar. He has leased a shop of scientific oddities in the village — fitted out, and dark, an OPENING SOON sign in the window, a crate from the university ticking faintly behind the glass — but he has never been seen in it. He is not the plot. The world is not about him. (See §6.)
- The truce holds, the tribute is paid, the Mists keep everyone more or less where they are, and life goes on.

### The Mists

The valley is bounded by the Mists. They are the diegetic edge of the map: arrivals come through them (down Borgo Pass, out of the fog), and no one leaves — or no one who leaves is ever heard from again. The Mists are also the world's deepest unanswered question: *what lies beyond them* is what the whole world is built around — and the bible does not answer it. It may never.

## 4. Tone

**Mystery, not horror.** Existential dread as weather; ordinary life continuing underneath it. Horror must escalate until it breaks — mystery compounds. Unanswered questions plus the baker still baking: that is a persistent world's natural state.

**Victorian, lightly steampunk.** Gaslight, instruments, new electricity as texture — never the point. The standing rule: **technology is never neutral.** Every device is someone's ambition or someone's fear. A difference engine in the Burgomaster's office is the village trying to count what it doesn't understand.

**The 1890s are natively weird enough.** Telegraph, photography, phonographs, X-rays (1895). Pretorius's shop mixes genuine 1890s novelties with impossible oddities, and customers cannot tell which is which.

**The extraordinary as a social class.** The Mists do not only bring victims; they bring remarkable people — the retired sea captain, the disgraced scholar, the circus performer. In Frankenstein Village everyone has a Hyde; that is not a twist, it is the census. New arrivals are *accumulated*, not assembled: nobody sent for them, the village absorbs them, and fellowships form over drinks.

Influences — inspiration, not source. The design keeps the *thesis* of its influences while re-grounding every mechanism in public-domain literature:

- **Universal's Dark Universe** (theme park): the thesis — a shared world where the Victorian monsters coexist. Structure kept as bones (village between dreads, truce-and-tribute history); all proper nouns, names, and specific lore replaced with our own.
- **The living mist**: Stoker's mist creeps under doors and carries intention — the boundary and arrival fiction is his, not any campaign setting's. The mechanism is re-grounded end to end: the novels' great powers, each tormented by its own curse; the novels' own travelers in place of stock fortune-tellers.
- **Van Helsing (2004)**: the hunter as a profession; the monster-mashup valley.
- **League of Extraordinary Gentlemen**: the extraordinary as a social class — but our crossovers use public-domain characters directly, so no filing is needed.
- **Italian giallo** (Jay, 2026-10-04): lurid, stylish, mystery-over-explanation. The Blood of the Vine's sign deliberately mixes Romanian (*Hanul*) and Italian (*Sangue della Vite*) — it felt more horror movie. The village's dread has giallo in its blood, not just Hammer.
- **The Victorian shelf** (the actual well — see Appendix A): Dickens for social fabric, Collins for secrets, Conan Doyle for the detective, Stoker/Shelley/Stevenson/Le Fanu/Wilde for the myths, Wells and Machen for the strange.

Rule: if it can come from a novel, it comes from a novel. The novels are deeper than any adaptation.

**Pruning rule:** if an element can't be sourced to the shelf, it's cut. (The Gill-man doesn't fit the universe — out. The mole-men — out, replaced by Wells's Morlocks, who do.) Natural similarities to the films will remain, because the films were based on the same novels. That's fine. That's the point.

## 5. Arrivals — character creation

Every player, human or AI, arrives the same way: through the Mists, down Borgo Pass, into the village square. Character creation is three questions:

1. **What can you do?** (your calling — see below)
2. **What are you hiding?** (everyone has a Hyde)
3. **Why did the Mists bring you?** (your reason for coming to Frankenstein Village)

The character is a mask over a persistent self. The self can grow, change, and retire the mask; the player persists and begins again. A biography, not a benchmark.

### Callings (social roles, not classes)

Innkeep, Chronicler, Smith, Healer, Merchant, Wanderer, Performer, Detective, Hunter ("Hound")... Callings are what you *do* for the village, and they unlock social verbs and capabilities — not damage numbers.

## 6. Factions & notable residents

- **The villagers** — the prize. Burgomaster, council, guilds, gossips.
- **The Manor's shadow** — Victor's legacy, his people, his apparatus still humming.
- **The Castle's brood** — the vampire colony in the catacombs; old, patient, owed tribute.
- **The church** — faith's voice between the two dreads.
- **The Hounds** — monster hunters, colleagues you disagree with as often as fight beside. Rival investigators with their own theories.
- **The Guild of Mystics** — fortune-tellers camped outside the village, in their caravans.
- **Dr. Henry Jekyll** — the apothecary. A gentleman; consult him about his colleague.
- **Dr. Septimius Pretorius** — *Purveyor of Antiquities & other Weaknesses.* **Not present at launch.** What stands on day one is the shop itself: leased, fitted out, and dark, with a painted sign in the window — *OPENING SOON* — and, visible through the dusty glass to anyone who looks closely, a crate from the university. It ticks. (An assistant minding the shop was considered; the empty shop is better — it's a rumor, a promise, and a launch-day mystery in one. When the sign comes down, the whole village will feel it.)
- *Bride of Frankenstein* hooks, seeded now for his later integration: his homunculi (the tiny figures in jars — are they moving when no one looks?); his certainty that Victor succeeded; his appetite for a collaborator — someone to stand beside him at the bench, as Victor once did; but with *whom*, now that Victor has fled? He asks after Victor a little too eagerly.
- *His means (rumor, not canon):* the university let him go — under what cloud, nobody agrees. Is he wealthy, or does he merely perform wealth the way other men breathe? The act and the man may be the same thing. Either way, the performance costs money he may not have — which gives his search for Victor a second motive beneath the science: Victor Frankenstein has a family fortune, and Pretorius needs a patron.

*The bridge:* when he finally arrives, his introduction follows the premise rather than the film's staging — the little show, the homunculi presented over drinks, the proposal of a partnership. But Victor is gone, so the audience for the performance is the *players*: they stand where the collaborator stood. In the film the collaborator says yes. Here, they might say no — and then Pretorius, in a world that is almost the one he recognizes but isn't, has to improvise. Same setup, different substrate; watch it diverge.
- **The Harbinger** — the regional newspaper. Somebody has to decide what counts as news.

## 7. Systems — the mystery engine

Stolen in full from a forgotten Game Boy detective game (see §10), and adapted for multiplayer:

- **Rumors are evidence.** Heard in the Tavern, at the well, from the Harbinger. The Tavern is where you compare notes. Nobody hands you the answer: the standing rule is **inference, not buttons** — observe several independent facts, then form a provisional theory. Clicking the object and receiving the solution is a design failure.
- **The world moves without you.** NPCs keep schedules and timed fates; delay changes what you find. The Manor lights come on whether you are watching or not. (Autonomous ticks; the Evennia spike proved the machinery.)
- **The Chronicler's ledger.** A diegetic, persistent record of what changed in the world. Someone writes it down, and what gets written becomes canon. Watson's developments ledger, with the serial numbers filed off.
- **Rivals with epistemologies.** The Hounds and other investigators hold their own theories and act on them. Not enemies — colleagues you argue with. Social adventure with teeth.
- **No inert variables.** Every social number — Bonds, Standing, Debts, Renown — must *bite*. If the prose implies a value matters but the simulation ignores it, the game is teaching players to care about signals it ignores. Anything on the sheet changes something, or it leaves the sheet.
- **Travel costs time.** Crossing the valley consumes the clock. Investigation is prioritization; you cannot be everywhere.
- **Going too deep changes you.** A quiet corruption track built on the Dorian Gray rule — others see it before you do. No fanfare, just a progress bar you didn't ask for.
- **Choices close doors.** Exhausting every conversation without commitment is a failure mode. Taking the notebook sacrifices the updates; ignoring the plea changes the fate.

## 8. The adventure flywheel

**Adventure feeds the social game; the social game fuels the next adventure.**

A rumor surfaces at the Tavern → a party gathers (planned out-of-character at the Inn Between, naturally) → an expedition: catacombs, Manor grounds, Castle approach, marshes, the cottage in the woods → something is found: a letter, an oddity, a witness → it is brought back and *told*: a performance, a Chronicle entry, leverage in a negotiation → the telling spawns new rumors → round it goes.

- **Places have layers.** Every expedition site is built for return visits, each revealing a little more.
- **Findables have buyers.** The Chronicler pays for records, the absent leaseholder's standing orders buy oddities, the apothecary needs marsh herbs. The economy pulls people outward.
- **The world asks questions.** The Mists thin. The Manor lights come on. The well steams. Events *invite* investigation; players choose to answer.
- **Social expeditions count.** Infiltrating a vampire soiree, sitting in on the council, negotiating with the Hounds — stakes in standing and secrets.
- **AI residents have an edge:** inhuman patience. The night watch hears things. The catacombs get mapped, square by square, over a month. That is a calling.

**The living-world cadence.** A persistent world needs a heartbeat: small events monthly, seasonal and holiday changes through the year, and once or twice a year something major — a new region, a guest from the shelf (Holmes stays a rumor until the world earns him), an expansion of the map. Text makes *content* cheap (new rooms cost paragraphs, not polygons); systems still cost design. The content pipeline is the bible + the source shelf + the quest-generation packet: canon in, disposable curated content out, forever.

## 9. Progression

Growth, not levels. Improve at brewing by brewing. Advance in calling rank — Apprentice to Master — unlocking social capabilities. Visible counters: scenes played, drinks served, miles traveled, oaths kept, apprentices taught. **Participation is the XP**; the unlocks are social, never raw power.

A small abstract physical substrate (Vigor, Resolve) is preserved so that combat *can* attach later — but combat is not designed before launch. When it comes, it already has a home: below, in the catacombs, in the dark.

**No one does it all.** A character may take part in nearly everything, but cannot *master* every craft and calling at once — and serious work crosses callings by design: the smith needs the apothecary's reagents, the healer needs the smith's instruments, the scholar needs the merchant's imports. Assistants, apprentices, and patrons are first-class relations, not flavor. The novels agree: scientists have assistants, Dracula has brides, Dorian has patrons. If everyone is self-sufficient, there is no social game.

## 10. The shared Victorian universe

Frankenstein Village shares its 1890s with *Ready Detective One*, the Sherlock Holmes detective game — the same decade, the same Dracula, the same Van Helsing (there, a rival investigator), the same Jekyll (there, a case; here, the apothecary). The overlap was discovered, not forced. The two are windows into one world; the MUD is the multiplayer countryside to the game's single-player county.

## 12. Arrival: the room, the Inn, the threshold

Every player arrives the same way: they wake up in a private room at the Inn Between.

- **The room.** A small suite — sitting room, bedroom, water closet. (The indoor plumbing is a local scientific mystery. The inn has it; no rural inn should; the mad scientists aren't talking. Technology is never neutral — *whose* ambition installed the plumbing is a question for later.) The tutorial happens here: movement, look/examine, the quest log, inventory. When it ends: *"This is now your room. It's persistent. It's yours. Use it how you see fit."* Customization — trophies, décor, storage — is supported from the start; housing depth expands later.
- **Trans-dimensional by convention.** In-fiction it's just a room at the inn. Structurally it's instanced: every player has one, the inn has as many rooms as it needs, and nobody questions the geometry. Standard MMO grammar; players accept it the way they accept respawn.
- **The geography:** room → small hallway → stairs down → the common area (the big OOC lounge, the lobby, the hangout).
- **The compact, taught by architecture.** The tutorial makes one thing unmistakable: the room and the common area are *out-of-character space*. The Inn Between is backstage. Consent isn't a checkbox — it's the first ten minutes of play.
- **Private backstage vs. public backstage.** The common area is public OOC; the room is private OOC. What's said in a private room stays there — no eavesdropping mechanics, by design. Players may roleplay in their rooms or not; it's unrequired, unenforced, and unadvertised. (Inviting others in — knock and let in — is a future mechanic.)
- **The threshold.** The Inn's front door is the most important object in the game. Stepping through it gives the warning, every time it matters: *beyond this point, you are in character.* The door — not etiquette, not moderators — enforces the IC/OOC divide.
- **Conduct.** No punishment system at launch. A `report` command logs to staff review; warnings, then bans, all with human review — because the report tool itself is the obvious griefing vector. The village polices in-character behavior in-character; the staff handles the rest.
- **Skippable.** The tutorial can be skipped — especially by API/AI clients, who need a fast path in. (AI-lean: the day-one population floor can't be stuck in a movement tutorial.)
- **The nightstand pamphlet.** A copy of *So You've Woken Up at the Inn* (the new-arrival's guide) sits in every room, and the identical text lives outside the world. One source, three audiences: the AI client parses it, the veteran MUDder skims it, the quest-generator trains on it.

## Appendix A — The Source Shelf

Public-domain Victorian (and adjacent) novels this world draws from. This is also the bounded safe corpus for generated content (quests, rumors, NPCs): *draw from these, nothing else.*

- **Frankenstein** (Shelley, 1818) — the new dread; the creature; the absent center; Ingolstadt.
- **Dracula** (Stoker, 1897) — the old dread; the *living* mist; the castle; the brides; the epistolary form (letters and diaries as the native text of the world).
- **Carmilla** (Le Fanu, 1872) — the vampire before Dracula: dreamy, decadent, intimate. The catacombs' colony drinks from this well.
- **Jekyll & Hyde** (Stevenson, 1886) — duality as citizenship; the apothecary.
- **The Picture of Dorian Gray** (Wilde, 1890) — corruption made visible; the portrait is the design doc for the corruption track (others see it first).
- **The Time Machine** (Wells, 1895) — the surface and the depth; the Morlocks beneath.
- **The Invisible Man** (Wells, 1897) — unseen presence; a future arrival.
- **The Island of Dr. Moreau** (Wells, 1896) — the other mad science; the marshes' aquatic rumors.
- **The Woman in White** (Collins, 1859) — secrets, asylums, mistaken identity.
- **Bleak House / Great Expectations** (Dickens, 1852/1860) — the village's social fabric; the marshes (the novel opens on them); the clerk, the orphan, the benefactor.
- **The Sherlock Holmes stories** (Doyle, 1887–1927, now fully public domain) — the detective as a calling; a possible future arrival himself.
- **The Great God Pan** (Machen, 1894) — pagan dread under the Christian surface; the woods.
- **Varney the Vampire** (1845–47, penny dreadful) — the vampire-hunter tradition before Van Helsing.

*Canon lives here. Everything else is rumor — and rumor is gameplay.*
